# Essay

125 marks · 1000–1200 words · two essays in three hours.

## What the essay actually rewards

Not information density. An essay is judged on **breadth of perspective, coherence, and whether it travels somewhere**. A candidate who dumps GS material into an essay scores below one who takes a narrower claim and develops it with genuine movement from beginning to end.

The single most useful structural instinct: every movement should *change the reader's angle* on the theme. If two consecutive sections make the same kind of point, one of them is padding.

## Sections are movements, not parts

Do not produce "introduction / body / conclusion" as sub-headings. The sections are the argumentative movements — aim for **5–7**, each one earning its place by shifting the argument. A workable arc:

1. The idea as commonly understood
2. Where that understanding comes from — its history or its appeal
3. The first crack: a case it does not explain
4. The deeper tension underneath
5. What resolves or reframes it
6. What follows for how we should act
7. Return to the opening image, now meaning something different

That is one arc, not a template. Derive the real one from the prompt.

## Framework library

| Purpose | Lens |
|---|---|
| Broad sweep | **Dimensions** — social · political · economic · ethical · environmental · technological · psychological |
| Movement through time | Past → present → emerging future |
| Widening the frame | Individual → family → community → nation → world |
| Two-sided prompts | **Thesis → antithesis → synthesis** |
| Problem-shaped prompts | Problem → cause → consequence → resolution |
| Abstract or philosophical prompts | Descriptive → analytical → normative: what is, why it is, what ought to be |
| Quote prompts | Literal reading → deeper reading → the case where it fails → the reconciled reading |
| Comparative prompts | What each gets right → what each misses → the frame that holds both |

Pick lenses that let the essay *move*. A dimensional sweep alone produces a list; combine it with a temporal or normative arc so the essay has direction.

## Intro types that suit the essay

Anecdote or vignette · a parable or folk tale · a quote (accurately attributed) · a sharp paradox · a historical moment · a concrete image the essay will return to at the end.

The strongest openings are **concrete**. A specific scene earns more attention than an abstract proposition, and gives the conclusion something to close.

## Conclusion types that suit the essay

Return to the opening image, transformed · a forward-looking vision · a call to a specific change rather than a general one · reconciliation of the tension the essay has built.

Never introduce a new argument. Never end on a quote that does the work the essay failed to do.

## Value addition

Range is the point. Draw examples from **more than one discipline**: literature, science, history, economics, cinema, personal observation. Quotes should span traditions — Indian and non-Indian, ancient and modern. Thinkers from more than one civilisation.

Avoid the examples every essay uses. If a reference would appear in half the papers the examiner reads that day, it adds nothing.

Keywords worth having: moral imagination · path dependence · tragedy of the commons · false dichotomy · unintended consequences · social capital · collective action problem · the paradox of choice · creative destruction · intergenerational equity.

## Tags

Tags use the topic **Themes**. Tag into every theme the essay could be turned toward — a good essay skeleton is reusable across several prompts, and the tags are how it gets found again.

## Diagram

Usually unnecessary. Return `""` unless a genuine 2×2 or a two-pole spectrum clarifies the theme in a way prose cannot. An essay is prose; a diagram in it draws attention to itself.

## Recurring essay traps

- Turning the essay into a GS answer with headings and bullet-style paragraphs.
- Breadth without direction — seven dimensions, no argument.
- Announcing the structure ("In this essay I will…") instead of enacting it.
- A conclusion that summarises rather than lands.
- Quotes stacked as decoration, unconnected to the sentence around them.
- Choosing the safe reading of a provocative prompt, and so having nothing to argue.
