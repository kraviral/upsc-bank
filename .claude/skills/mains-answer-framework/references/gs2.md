# GS2 — Polity, Governance, International Relations, Social Justice

Constitution and federalism · separation of powers · Parliament and state legislatures · executive and judiciary · constitutional and statutory bodies · governance, transparency and accountability · welfare schemes and vulnerable sections · India's foreign relations.

## What GS2 actually rewards

The marks sit in the **gap between text and practice**. A question that names a tension — autonomy against accountability, uniformity against diversity, speed against due process — is asking the candidate to sit inside that tension, not to resolve it by listing provisions. Reciting Articles is the floor, not the answer.

## Framework library

### Core lenses

| Purpose | Lens |
|---|---|
| Almost any institutional question | **Provision → judicial interpretation → practice gap** — the single most reliable GS2 lens |
| How an institution performs | Institution → process → outcome |
| Who is affected | Stakeholder map: Centre · State · local body · citizen · judiciary · private actor · civil society |
| Law against reality | De jure vs de facto — what the text says against what the data shows |
| Diagnosing a failure | Structural → functional → behavioural causes |
| Rights questions | Rights vs duties; individual liberty vs collective welfare; negative vs positive liberty |
| Reform proposals | Legal → institutional → procedural → attitudinal |
| Federal questions | Legislative · administrative · financial dimensions of the relationship |
| Accountability | Answerability → enforceability → responsiveness |
| Timeframe | Immediate → short term → long term |

### International relations

- **Interests → values → capabilities** — what India wants, what it stands for, what it can actually do
- **Bilateral → regional → multilateral** concentric circles
- Realist vs liberal vs constructivist readings of the same event
- Strategic autonomy vs alignment; issue-based coalitions
- Soft power, hard power, and the gap between them
- Continuity vs change across governments — foreign policy questions almost always reward showing both

## Value addition

- **Articles**, cited exactly. A wrong Article number is worse than none — mark `[verify]` if unsure.
- **Landmark judgments** named with the *principle* they established, not just the case name.
- **Committee and commission reports** — Sarkaria, Punchhi, 2nd ARC, Law Commission, Finance Commission.
- **Schemes** with what they actually do, not just their expansion.
- **Indices and rankings**, with the caveat that most are contested — say who publishes them.
- **Keywords**: constitutional morality · basic structure · cooperative and competitive federalism · subsidiarity · doctrine of pleasure · colourable legislation · judicial overreach vs judicial review · administrative discretion · capture · last-mile delivery · rights-based approach.

## Intro types that suit GS2

Constitutional provision or Preambular value · a landmark judgment's one-line principle · recent institutional event · a data point that frames the problem · Ambedkar or a Constituent Assembly debate quote · a stated paradox between two constitutional goals.

## Conclusion types that suit GS2

Constitutional-morality linkage · a specific institutional reform · committee recommendation as the way forward · SDG 16 · cooperative federalism as the frame · an aspirational picture of the institution working as designed.

## Diagram

Institutional flowchart · Centre–State–local pyramid · 2×2 of autonomy against accountability · stakeholder web · a bill's passage as a flow · separation-of-powers triangle with the checks labelled on each edge.

## Recurring GS2 traps

- Listing provisions without engaging the tension the question names.
- Treating a judgment as settling a question the political system is still fighting over.
- One-sided answers on federalism — almost every such question rewards both the centralising and the devolutionary reading.
- IR answers that become country profiles instead of arguments.
- Recommending "awareness" and "capacity building" as though they were mechanisms.
- Confusing constitutional bodies with statutory ones. Check before asserting.
